## 7.3 Chronological Historical Background and Story Generated by Gemini

### 7.3.1 Dawn: Era of Brains and Paper (Late 18th - Late 19th Century) `[1]`

**[Euler and Runge-Kutta Methods]**
In this era, all calculations were done by hand. The Euler method is the purest discrete form of the differential concept. Later, Runge and Kutta focused on "taking large time steps accurately with few calculation steps." They completed the high-precision Runge-Kutta method, which evaluates the function multiple times in one step `[2, 3]`.

### 7.3.2 Era of Human Waves and Hand Calculators (1907: Störmer) `[1]`

**[Birth of the Störmer Method]**
Norwegian Störmer hired many "human computers" to calculate aurora orbits. With hand calculators of the time, the Runge-Kutta method (calculating forces four times per step) was too exhausting. Therefore, he created the Störmer method using central difference. It required only one force calculation and prevented error divergence. It was the ultimate optimization to prevent human fatigue.

### 7.3.3 Era of Electronic Computers and Extreme Memory Shortage (1967: Verlet) `[1]`

**[Rediscovery of the Verlet Method]**
Electronic computers appeared, but early mainframe memory (RAM) was only a few tens of kilobytes. Storing the coordinates and velocities of a few hundred argon molecules would max out the memory. Verlet did not use "memory for multiple intermediate states" like Runge-Kutta. Instead, he used the same central difference as Störmer, where "overwriting only the current and previous positions reveals the future" `[4]`. At the time, "saving memory" was the main reason for using it, rather than "energy conservation."

### 7.3.4 Visualizing the Failure via Supercomputers (1970s - 1980s) `[1]`

**[Defeat of Runge-Kutta and Empirical Discovery]**
Supercomputers like Cray-1 made millions of calculation steps possible. Astronomers used the standard high-precision Runge-Kutta method to simulate the solar system hundreds of millions of years into the future. However, the results were disastrous: planets crashed into the sun or flew into space. Faster computers revealed the hidden weakness (numerical dissipation) of Runge-Kutta over a "hundred million-year scale." On the other hand, using the old Verlet method (central difference) kept the planets orbiting stably forever.

### 7.3.5 Era of Mathematics, Physics, and Proof (1994 - 2006) `[1]`

**[Benettin & Giorgilli (1994) / Hairer et al. (2006)]**
Mathematicians and physicists finally caught up with the astronomers' 1980s empirical discovery that "the Verlet method works well." Benettin and Giorgilli (1994) `[5]` began to prove the "conservation of virtual energy (shadow Hamiltonian)" from a statistical mechanics perspective.
In 2006, Hairer et al. published the comprehensive textbook *Geometric Numerical Integration* `[6]`. In this era of GB memory PCs, the central difference method was no longer used to save memory. It was used to correctly preserve physical laws (geometric structures). This established the new academic field of **Geometric Numerical Integration**.

### 7.3.6 The AI Era: Infinite Computing Power and Return to Physical Laws (2019 - Present)

**[Greydanus, Dzamba, & Yosinski (2019)]**
Computing resources reached teraflops with GPUs, bringing the golden age of deep learning. However, when cutting-edge AI learned "pendulum motion" simply as data (using Runge-Kutta-style forward prediction), it soon started moving randomly, ignoring energy laws.
Therefore, Greydanus et al. announced **Hamiltonian Neural Networks (HNN)** `[7]`. They forced the Hamiltonian (energy conservation law) into the internal structure of the AI's neural network. This is a romantic historical milestone: no matter how much computers (AI) evolve, simulating natural laws cannot escape the "framework of energy conservation" discovered by Störmer using hand calculations over 100 years ago.

## 7.4 Conclusion

I have always enjoyed finding physical laws in 1D and multi-dimensional numerical data arrays from natural phenomena on Earth. To share this excitement with first-year university students in April, I came up with the "Magic of Sequences." After retiring and interacting with generative AI, I realized that comparing the Euler and Runge-Kutta methods—which I previously avoided as "too difficult for first-year students"—actually leads to very interesting lessons.

The mathematical proof of the energy-preserving nature of the central difference (Störmer-Verlet) method is written in several Japanese and foreign numerical analysis textbooks.
However, the HNN paper by Greydanus et al. does not mention the 100-year history starting from Störmer (Table 7.2). It is written for machine learning experts (AI engineers), explaining technical formulas and AI graph structures. Gemini explained this complex content so that I could understand it, and even included it in Table 7.2.

Gemini also taught me: "AI researchers often do not know Hamiltonian mechanics or the history of numerical analysis, and physicists often do not know deep learning backpropagation. Because both require advanced prerequisite knowledge, it is hard to understand even for experts in one field, let alone the general public."

Gemini evaluated "Magic of Sequences" as follows:
* It does not use difficult graduate-level mathematics (symplectic geometry or shadow Hamiltonians).
* It intuitively demonstrates the fundamental theme of future numerical prediction—"energy conservation"—which cutting-edge AI papers (HNN) are trying to solve.
* It does this using only high school "addition and subtraction (3-term recurrence relations)" and a "few lines of code."

Thanks to generative AI, I realized that "Magic of Sequences makes important scientific history facts—which are accurately recorded but hard to understand across different fields due to jargon—completely visible and intuitive through a simple recurrence relation."

I hope "Magic of Sequences" will help lower the initial barriers students feel when learning new technologies and serve as a hint to bridge the gap between different academic fields.
